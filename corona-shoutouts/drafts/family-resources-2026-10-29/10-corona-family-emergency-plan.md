# Be Ready, Corona: A Family Guide to Fire Season & Emergencies

![A caregiver and child prepare emergency supplies with their dog nearby.](images/10-hero.jpg)

*AI-generated editorial illustration; depicts a conceptual scene, not a specific local person, place or program.*

Corona families can prepare now by enrolling in **Corona Alerts**, checking **Alert RivCo and Genasys Protect**, and making an evacuation plan that includes children and pets. Keep essential supplies ready and decide who handles pickup and transportation. During an incident, follow current official instructions rather than waiting for a neighborhood message.

An emergency plan is most useful when it answers ordinary questions before an extraordinary day: Who gets the children? Where is the cat carrier? How do we leave if the usual route is unavailable? Work through those questions together while there is time.

## Sign up for the alerts that cover your home

The [Corona Alerts signup page](https://public.alertsense.com/SignUp/?RegionId=2123) offers city notifications through options including text, email and telephone, depending on preferences and the incident. Entering an address helps target messages to the area that matters to your household.

The city says the service covers emergencies requiring action, such as evacuation or shelter-in-place instructions, along with some significant non-emergency disruptions. It is free, although standard messaging or other carrier charges may apply. Keep your contact details current if you move or change numbers.

[Riverside County Emergency Management](https://emd.rivco.gov/alert-rivco) encourages residents to register for both **Alert RivCo** and **Protect** as additional ways to receive warnings. Its page specifically notes that some cities, including Corona, operate their own systems and advises checking those as well.

The county explains that cellphones and internet-based telephone numbers are not automatically enrolled in the same way as traditional landlines. Do not assume a mobile phone is registered because you receive other government messages. Visit the official registration page and verify your household’s setup.

## Decide who does what if you need to leave

Choose the adult responsible for each immediate task: collecting children, gathering medications, loading pets and checking the official route information. Decide what happens if one caregiver is at work or cannot get home. A plan that depends on everyone being together needs a backup.

Review your children’s school or childcare emergency procedures. Confirm authorized pickup contacts and discuss a backup adult who can act if roads or work obligations prevent you from arriving. Follow the school’s instructions during an actual incident rather than assuming ordinary pickup arrangements still apply.

Pick a nearby meeting place and another option outside the immediate area. Share an out-of-area contact who can help family members reconnect if local communication is difficult. Write important telephone numbers on paper as well as storing them in phones.

Identify more than one possible way out of your neighborhood, but use routes directed by officials during the incident. A route you know from everyday driving may be closed or unsafe during a fire. Do not rely on a navigation app as the only source of emergency road information.

## Pack essentials you can actually carry

[Ready.gov’s emergency-kit guidance](https://www.ready.gov/kit) recommends water, food and basic supplies for several days. Its water guidance uses **at least one gallon per person per day** for drinking and sanitation. Plan additional supplies for pets and household needs.

A home supply kit and a bag you can take quickly serve different purposes. Keep the portable bag manageable, with essentials such as medications, important-document copies, phone charging equipment, a flashlight, basic first-aid supplies and clothing suited to the household.

Include items that make children’s needs easier to meet: diapers if needed, a familiar comfort item, snacks and written caregiver contact information. Check sizes, expiration dates and medication instructions regularly. A bag packed years ago may no longer fit the family using it.

For a person who uses medical equipment, plan for power, charging, transport and necessary supplies with the relevant care team or provider. Keep essential information available to caregivers. Do not wait until the power is out to discover how long a device can operate without charging.

## Give pets their own part of the plan

[CAL FIRE’s evacuation guidance](https://www.readyforwildfire.org/prepare-for-wildfire/go-evacuation-guide) recommends preparing pets to leave with carriers, food and water. Keep leashes and carriers accessible, and know which adult will secure each animal rather than assigning everyone to look for the same pet.

Include identification and veterinary records, medications, bowls and appropriate cleanup supplies. Check that tags and contact details are current. Practice getting a reluctant animal into its carrier under calm conditions so the carrier is familiar before an evacuation.

Choose potential destinations that accept your animals and verify arrangements during an incident. Do not assume every shelter or hotel can take every pet. If you depend on friends or relatives, discuss the plan with them in advance.

For horses or other large animals, transportation requires earlier planning: a trailer, a capable driver, loading practice and a destination. CAL FIRE warns that firefighters may not be able to evacuate livestock. Arrange assistance before conditions become urgent and allow extra time.

## Know the difference between preparing and waiting too long

An evacuation warning means prepare to leave and consider leaving early, particularly if people or animals need additional time. An evacuation order means leave as directed. Follow the exact language and geographic instructions issued by local authorities for the incident.

CAL FIRE cautions that officials may not have time for door-to-door warnings during a fast-moving wildfire. Do not make a personal knock at the door part of your departure requirement. If conditions feel unsafe, seek current official guidance and leave early when it is safe to do so.

Keep keys, shoes and necessary mobility equipment accessible. If power outages could affect your garage door or gate, learn the safe manual operation in advance. Maintain your vehicle’s readiness so fueling or charging is not a last-minute obstacle.

Do not delay departure to complete a long household checklist or collect replaceable belongings. The family plan should make leaving simpler. If a task cannot be done without losing safe departure time, prioritize people and animals and follow emergency instructions.

## Practice one short version this week

Walk through the plan without creating a frightening scenario. Ask each person where the meeting place is, who the backup contact is and which items they are responsible for. Young children need a simple instruction they can remember, not a lecture about every possible emergency.

Try loading the essential bags and pet carriers, then identify what slowed you down. Move supplies if necessary and update the written plan. A brief practice can reveal that the important folder is hard to find or one adult does not know where the spare keys are.

Finally, check in with a neighbor or relative who may need assistance and agree on a realistic way to help. Sign up for alerts today, then finish one practical task tomorrow. Share this Corona Shoutouts guide so more households have their plan ready before they need it.
